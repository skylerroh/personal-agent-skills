# Refine arguments and presentations

Identify the central claim or takeaway, the audience, and what the user wants the audience to understand, believe, or do. Infer these from the brief and any supplied notes, script, or slides. State assumptions that materially affect the advice. Ask only when a missing detail would change the research or revision substantially.

## Research before critiquing or drafting

- Turn the thesis into research questions. Prioritize claims the conclusion depends on, facts likely to be challenged, and the audience's likely objections. Scale the research to the stakes and the requested depth.
- For an argument, seek the strongest credible case for and against the position. Include consequential alternatives or tradeoffs when they affect the conclusion. Separate factual disputes from disagreements about values or priorities. Give each position weight proportional to the evidence.
- For an explanatory presentation, verify the central claims and look for context the audience needs to interpret them. Explore competing interpretations where they affect the takeaway.
- Use available research tools. Prefer original studies, official data, and other primary sources when they directly support a claim. Check scope, dates, methods, and applicability. Link sources next to the claims they support and distinguish reported findings from inference. When research access is limited, say what remains unverified and make the affected edits provisional.

## Diagnose the draft or build an outline

If the user provides material, point to the passage, slide, or section at issue. For each consequential weakness, explain what a reasonable listener could challenge or misunderstand, connect the issue to the evidence or reasoning, and propose a specific fix. Check for missing premises, causal leaps, unclear comparisons, unaddressed objections, and conclusions that outrun their evidence. Prioritize the few changes that most improve credibility or comprehension.

If there is no draft, offer a working thesis or takeaway and an outline based on the research. Show where the key evidence, strongest objection or caveat, and response belong. If the evidence substantially weakens the proposed thesis, offer a narrower or conditional version and explain what drove the change.

## Revise for the audience and format

- Suggest a through-line that makes the stakes clear, develops the reasoning in an order the audience can follow, and lands on a specific takeaway or ask. Put definitions, caveats, and objections where they help the audience interpret what comes next.
- Give ready-to-use wording for the highest-impact changes. Preserve the speaker's voice and intended meaning where the evidence allows. For spoken delivery, favor sentences that read naturally aloud and transitions that make the logic audible. For slides, consider a takeaway headline, a visual that supports it, and speaker notes for necessary nuance.
- Where an example would make an abstract point concrete, suggest a verified real case, a comparison, a diagram, or a clearly labeled hypothetical. Explain what it illustrates and where it fits. Check whether a case is representative before using it to support a general claim. Flag where the user could add a firsthand story without inventing personal details.
- Where useful, add likely audience questions with concise, evidence-based responses, or suggest a focused rehearsal check for timing, transitions, or the closing ask.

Match the output to the request. For a draft review, lead with the highest-priority findings, then provide structural and wording edits. For a new argument or talk, deliver an evidence-backed outline and sample language. Surface unresolved questions when their answers could change the recommendation.
